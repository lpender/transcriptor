# The refined idea (2026-10-04)

> **Deck v2 was skipped deliberately.** Round 1 did not produce an edit to the
> same pitch; it produced a different buyer. Rewriting v1 with better slides
> would have polished the wrong deck. v1 → teardown → v3.

## The position, in one sentence a competitor could not also write

**Tablework is the producing organisation's rehearsal record: one shared
reading of the play, and a season's worth of evidence about which actors
actually go off book.**

Not "an AI scene partner". Not "a line-learning app". The competitors sell a
rehearsal aid to an actor. This sells a **record to the body that produces**
— a school theatre department, a community theatre, a conservatoire — which is
the only party in the building with a budget line, a next show, and a reason
to care who was off book last time.

## What changes in the product

Almost nothing in the code, and everything in what it says and charges.

| | Today | Refined |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | the cast, $1 a seat | the **organisation**, per production or per season |
| Unit | a production (dies when the show closes) | an **account that programmes four shows a year** |
| Price | $10/month for a cast of ten | **$199–399 a season** (unverified, needs the pricing test below) |
| What is sold | voices and line drills | the **record**: who is off book, how early, across shows |
| Churn | structural — the show ends | annual, against a department that produces every year |
| Already built | all of it | all of it. Productions already nest under an account; roles, billing and invites exist. |

The one genuine build this implies: **a season view** — the same off-book
standing, across productions, for the organisation rather than one show.
Everything it needs is already in D1 (`progress`, keyed by production and
member). That is days, not months, and it is the only new screen.

## What it is worth

See `tam-arithmetic.md`. The short form: at $1 a seat the absolute ceiling is
too small to pay for its own customers, and the entire question is whether the
organisation price clears a CAC that a two-channel founder can actually pay.

## Confirmed independently, after this was written

The market leg (`teardown-round-2.md`) landed after the pitch window closed and
found this position already occupied — profitably:

> **Stage Write**: free read-only seats for the cast, paid seats for the budget
> holder, **$249/year team, $599/year education**. The one commercially healthy
> product in a category where roughly 25 shipping apps are free, the price
> ceiling for an individual has been **$4 once since 2016**, and the leader by
> installed base (coldRead, 3,934 ratings) **froze development in 2022 while
> still billing $83.99 a year**.

Two consequences. **The buyer is proven**, so the pivot stops being a
hypothesis. And **the price band here was too low**: $249–599 a year, not
$199–399. A competitor already took the harder half of the risk and survived
it, which is the best possible news for this position and the worst possible
news for the $1-a-seat one.

## The counter-argument, kept on the page

**Who would conclude the opposite, and why they might be right:**

1. ~~**The solo-app founders.**~~ **Weakened by the market leg, and worth
   keeping for the record.** The argument was that four products selling to
   individual actors at $7–14/month proves the actor pays. The leg shows the
   opposite: those products are mostly frozen or dead, every currently-shipping
   line-learning app is free, and the individual's ceiling is $4 once. The
   actor does not pay. **But a harder counter replaced it:** *Scene Partner*
   was the only app with licensed MTI and Samuel French script text — the
   asset anyone would call this category's moat — and it died anyway, with no
   cause ever made public. Assets do not save products here.
2. **The founder's own channel is cast-shaped, not institution-shaped.** He
   knows actors and directors, not department heads and managing directors. A
   pivot to an institutional buyer discards the only distribution he owns, and
   the investor's recorded view is that the channel, not the product, is what
   almost nobody gets right.
3. **Education sales cycles are a season long.** A product priced per season
   gets one at-bat per year per account. With under six months of runway and a
   full-time job, that is a plan whose feedback loop is slower than the
   founder's attention span for it.

These are not disposed of. They are the reason the ask below is a test and not
a build.

## The ask, which is not money

Four things, none of which need capital, all of which can be done beside a
full-time job inside a month:

1. **Ask five directors in his network what the department actually pays for**
   — licensing, scripts, software, anything — and who signs. Five answers
   kills or confirms the institutional buyer outright.
2. **Instrument the invite link.** It is the only acquisition mechanic the
   product has and it is unmeasured. Count: invites sent, accepted, and how
   many accepters later start a production of their own. That number is the
   whole business.
3. **Charge the current production.** One real paying customer at any price
   answers more than the whole deck. This investor will not look at a company
   without revenue, and neither should the founder.
4. **Read the PIIA clause.** Before any of the above becomes commercial.

**What is explicitly not the ask: more features.** The perimeter — sound cues,
room sync — should get nothing. It is the part of the product that came from
the founder's own show rather than from a customer.
